What are Parametric Equations?

A parametric equation expresses a curve using a third variable, typically t, called a parameter. Instead of writing y directly as a function of x, we describe both x and y in terms of t: x = f(t), y = g(t) As t changes, the pair (x(t), y(t)) traces out a path in the coordinate plane.

Parametric equations have their roots in the development of analytic geometry in the 1600s by thinkers like Descartes and Fermat, but they became more widely used in the 18th and 19th centuries through the work of mathematicians such as Euler and Lagrange, who applied them to describe motion and solve problems in mechanics. Unlike standard equations that relate x and y directly, parametric equations allow both to be defined in terms of a third variable, often time, making them ideal for modeling movement and complex curves. Today, parametric equations are used in physics to describe projectile motion and orbital paths, in engineering for designing mechanisms and trajectories, in robotics for path planning, in computer graphics for animation and camera movement, in medicine for analyzing joint motion, and in architecture for creating intricate shapes using CAD software.

Why Use Parametric Equations?

Parametric equations give us more control over how a curve is traced out. They're especially useful for:

  • Describing motion (position over time)
  • Modeling curves that loop, reverse, or break vertical line tests
  • Describing paths in physics, engineering, and computer animation

Eliminating the Parameter

To convert a parametric equation into a Cartesian equation:

  1. Solve one equation (usually x = f(t)) for t.
  2. Substitute into the other equation to eliminate t.
